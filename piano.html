The 12 Key Pattern

A piano looks like a simple row of black and white keys. But the pattern repeats with a rule, and that rule even decides the shape of the piano.

Topics: translation, multiplying patterns, exponential functions, rotation

PART 1Notice + NameBlack keys in groups of 2 and 3 PART 2Explain the MathShift, double, and shape PART 3CreateMy Note Clock
1Notice + Name

What I noticed

Look at any piano. The white keys are all the same, but the black keys are not spread evenly. They come in a group of 2, then a group of 3, then 2 again, then 3 again.

I started to notice this because I listen to Ludovico Einaudi a lot, and this week I am going to see him live. His music repeats small patterns again and again, and I wondered if the piano itself is built on a repeating pattern too.

It is. After every 12 keys (7 white and 5 black) the exact same picture starts again. A full piano has 88 keys: 52 white and 36 black.

Name it: this is a repeating pattern with translation. One block of 12 keys is shifted again and again along a line, like tiles on a floor but in one direction.

Questions I wanted to answer

  • Why does the pattern repeat every 12 keys and not 10 or 7?
  • What changes from one key to the next, is it adding or multiplying?
  • Why is a grand piano shaped like a curve and not a rectangle?
2Explain the Math

The math behind the pattern

A

The pattern is a translation

If I slide one octave 12 keys to the right, it lands exactly on the next octave. That is a translation: same shape, same size, just moved. The piano is 7 full blocks of 12 keys plus 4 extra keys at the top:

7 × 12 + 4 = 84 + 4 = 88 keys
B

Each key multiplies, it does not add

Every key makes a sound with a frequency (how many times the string shakes per second, in Hz). The middle A is 440 Hz. When you go one key up, the frequency does not go up by the same amount. It gets multiplied by the same number every time, about 1.0595.

Why that number? Because after 12 steps the sound has to be exactly double:

(21/12)12 = 2 so one step = 21/12 ≈ 1.0595

That gives a rule for every key on the piano. If n is how many keys you are away from the middle A:

f(n) = 440 · 2n/12n = 12 → 880 Hz (double) · n = −12 → 220 Hz (half) · Middle C is 9 keys below A: 440 · 2−9/12 ≈ 261.6 Hz

This answers my first question: the pattern repeats every 12 keys because after 12 multiplying steps the sound doubles, and our ears hear a doubled sound as "the same note, just higher". So the picture of the keys repeats too.
C

From sound to shape

Inside the piano every key hits a string. If strings are the same thickness and tightness, a string that is half as long makes a sound that is double the frequency. So going down one octave, the string should be twice as long. The top string is about 5 cm, so watch what happens:

No room has space for a 7.6 m piano. So piano makers cheat a little: the low strings are made thicker and wrapped with copper wire. A heavier string sounds lower, so it can be shorter.

But the lengths still grow faster and faster as you go to the low notes. If you look at a grand piano from above, the long curved side follows this growing curve. The exponential function from the sound becomes the shape of the wood.

This answers my last question: a grand piano is curved because string length grows like 2n/12, and the case is built around the strings.
3Create

My creation: the Note Clock

The pattern repeats every 12 keys, so after 12 steps you are "back home". That made me think of a clock. So I put the 12 notes of one octave around a circle like the 12 hours, 30° apart (360 ÷ 12 = 30).

My rule: start at C and keep jumping the same number of keys. Draw a line for every jump. Different jump sizes make totally different shapes:

JumpShape you get
1, 5, 7, 11one path that visits all 12 notes (5 and 7 make a 12 point star)
22 hexagons
33 squares
44 triangles
66 lines
number of shapes = gcd(jump, 12)
corners in each shape = 12 ÷ gcdgcd = greatest common divisor. 7 and 12 share no factor, so jumping 7 visits every note before coming back. Musicians call this path the circle of fifths.

Try it yourself

Move the slider to pick a jump size. Press play to hear the path.

Jump:7

Why I made these choices

A circle, not a line. On the keyboard the pattern goes on forever to the right. On a circle, going 12 steps brings you back to the start, which shows the "repeat every 12" idea in one picture.

30° between notes. 12 equal spaces in 360°. That gives the clock rotational symmetry: turn it 30° and every note lands on the next note.

Sound added. The play button uses the same formula from Part 2, f = 440 · 2n/12, so you can hear that the shapes are real music. Try jump 7 and you will hear the circle of fifths.

Something to wonder about

What if a piano had 10 keys per octave instead of 12? Which jumps would visit every note, and what shape would a jump of 4 make? (Hint: gcd(4, 10) = 2.)
