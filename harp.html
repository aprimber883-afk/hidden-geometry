The Shape of a Harp

A harp is a big triangle full of strings. Some are short, some are long, and the top is curved like a wave. The lengths are not random, they follow fractions.

Topics: ratios and fractions, inverse proportion, shape

PART 1Notice + NameShort to long, with a curved top PART 2Explain the MathFractions make music PART 3CreateMy 8 String Harp
1Notice + Name

What I noticed

A harp looks like a big triangle. All the strings are parallel to each other. On one side they are very short and on the other side they are very long. The top part, called the neck, is not straight, it curves like a wave.

This caught my attention because the strings change length step by step, like stairs. I wanted to know if there is a rule for how long each string should be, and why the top of the harp is curved instead of a straight line.

Name it: the harp is a pattern of parallel lines whose lengths follow ratios. The length of each string decides its sound.

Questions I wanted to answer

  • How does the length of a string decide its note?
  • Is there a math rule for the lengths, or did the builder just guess?
  • Why is the neck curved and not straight?
2Explain the Math

The math behind the harp

A

Pythagoras and fractions

About 2,500 years ago the Greek thinker Pythagoras did an experiment with one string. When he pressed it in the middle, so only 1/2 of the string could shake, he heard the same note but higher. Musicians call this an octave. Other simple fractions also sounded nice together:

B

Shorter string, higher sound: inverse proportion

If the strings are the same type and pulled with the same force, length and frequency follow one rule: when one goes up, the other goes down by the same factor. Their product stays the same:

f × L = constant → f = k ÷ LHalf the length → double the frequency. This is called inverse proportion.

Example: a 60 cm string that plays 220 Hz gives k = 220 × 60 = 13,200. Now I can find the sound of any length:

C

Why the neck is curved

A big concert harp has 47 strings and covers about 6 and a half octaves. Every octave lower needs a string twice as long. If the shortest string was 6 cm and the harp followed the rule exactly:

6 cm × 26.5 ≈ 6 × 90.5 ≈ 543 cm = about 5.4 meters!

But a real concert harp is less than 2 meters tall. So, like on a piano, harp makers make the low strings thicker and heavier (some are wrapped in metal wire) so they can be shorter and still sound low. The string tops do not line up in a straight line anymore. The curved neck is the shape you get when you connect the tops of all the strings.

This answers my questions: the lengths follow fractions and inverse proportion, and the curved neck is a "graph" of the string lengths made out of wood.
3Create

My creation: an 8 String Harp

I designed my own small harp that plays one full scale, Do Re Mi Fa Sol La Ti Do. I started with a 60 cm string and used the classic fractions for each note. To get each length I multiplied 60 cm by the fraction:

NoteFractionLengthFrequency
Do160 cm220 Hz
Re8/953.3 cm247.5 Hz
Mi4/548 cm275 Hz
Fa3/445 cm293.3 Hz
Sol2/340 cm330 Hz
La3/536 cm366.7 Hz
Ti8/1532 cm412.5 Hz
Do1/230 cm440 Hz

Each sound uses the formula from Part 2: f = 13200 ÷ L.

Why I made these choices

8 strings so the harp plays one full octave, from 60 cm to exactly half, 30 cm.

Fractions, not equal steps. I did not make each string a fixed number of cm shorter. I used the fractions from Pythagoras, because those are the lengths that sound right together.

The curved top. When I connected the tops of the strings, I got a curve, not a straight line. The first strings drop by 6.7 cm and 5.3 cm, but the last one only drops by 2 cm, because I used fractions and not equal steps. It is the same idea that gives a real harp its curved neck. You could build this harp with a wooden frame and fishing line.

Something to wonder about

If I wanted my harp to go one more octave higher, how long would the shortest string be? And if I wanted one octave lower, how long would the longest string be? Which one is harder to build, and why?
